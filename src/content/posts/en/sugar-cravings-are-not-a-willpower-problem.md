The problem probably isn't your discipline.

By late afternoon on a short-sleep day, the pull toward something sweet or salty can feel like a personal failing — proof you lack the self-control other people seem to have. The neuroendocrine research points somewhere else: your appetite hormones are running a different program than usual, and they started running it the night before.

## The Research

In a landmark controlled study, healthy young men spent two nights restricted to 4 hours of sleep and, on a separate occasion, two nights at 10 hours. The short-sleep condition produced measurable hormonal shifts: leptin — the hormone released by fat cells that signals the brain "you have enough energy, you can stop eating" — dropped by about 18%. Ghrelin — the hormone that signals hunger — rose by about 28%.

The behavioral effect tracked the biology. Subjective hunger increased by roughly 24% overall, but the appetite for calorie-dense, high-carbohydrate foods — sweets, salty snacks, starchy foods — rose disproportionately, by 33 to 45%. The body wasn't just asking for more food in general. It was asking, specifically, for the foods that deliver the fastest energy.

This is a hormonal appetite signal operating on incomplete information. Ghrelin and leptin evolved to track energy balance across a day, not to account for a night of interrupted or shortened sleep — so when sleep debt accumulates, the system reads it as an energy shortfall and escalates the hunger signal accordingly. The craving you feel by 3pm after a short night isn't a mystery of weak resolve; it's a hormone doing exactly what it's built to do, in response to bad information.

## The Japanese Lens

There's an old piece of dietary wisdom in Japan: *hara hachi bu* (腹八分目) — eat until you're 80% full. It's usually taught as a practice of restraint, a discipline you cultivate at the table. But it quietly assumes something: that your hunger signal is trustworthy enough to stop at 80% in the first place.

That assumption holds up on a well-rested night and breaks down on a short one. Practicing restraint against a hunger signal that's been artificially inflated by sleep loss isn't discipline — it's fighting biology with willpower alone, which is a losing trade most nights. The more reliable lever isn't more resolve at the table. It's the sleep that keeps the signal accurate before you ever sit down.

## The Lab Note

Next time an afternoon sugar craving hits harder than usual, check the night before it, not your character. If you were short on sleep, notice that the craving is loud in proportion — and that protecting one full night tends to turn the volume back down on its own.
