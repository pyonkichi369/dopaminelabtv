Your brain didn't decide to punish you this morning.

For the first minutes after waking, most people are, measurably, worse at thinking than they were the night before — slower reaction times, foggier judgment, a strong pull back toward the pillow. It's easy to read that as evidence you're "just not a morning person." The research says something more specific: it isn't your type. It's a stage everyone passes through.

## The Research

Sleep researchers call it sleep inertia — a transitional state between sleep and full wakefulness, marked by grogginess, slowed reaction time, and reduced short-term memory and decision-making. It's driven by the way different brain regions wake up on different schedules. The prefrontal cortex, responsible for planning and self-control, tends to lag behind more primitive arousal systems, and slow-wave activity — the same electrical pattern that dominates deep sleep — persists there even after your eyes are open.

Most people clear the worst of it within 15 to 30 minutes. It's longer and heavier if you're waking from deep sleep rather than light sleep, if you're sleep-deprived, or if an alarm cuts you off mid-cycle. None of that is a character flaw. It's closer to a cold engine — it runs, but not at full output until it warms up.

Chronotype — whether you naturally lean toward early or late hours — is a separate system. It shapes when your body wants to sleep and wake, not how much fog you experience in the minutes after the alarm. A dedicated early riser can still wake up useless if the timing or depth of their sleep was off; a self-described night owl can wake up sharp if the conditions were right. Conflating the two turns a fixable morning problem into a fixed identity.

What does help, consistently, is light. Circadian research shows that a week of exposure to only natural light — no phones, no indoor lamps — is enough to resynchronize the body's internal clock to the sun, with the earliest risers among us showing the biggest shift. The same signaling pathway that pulls your circadian clock into alignment with daylight is active the moment light reaches your eyes in the morning, which is consistent with why getting outside, or at least near a bright window, tends to move people out of the fog faster than sitting in dim indoor light waiting to "feel ready."

## The Japanese Lens

Traditional Japanese arts — tea ceremony, budo, calligraphy — share a teaching often summarized as *kata kara hairu* (型から入る): enter through form. You don't wait to feel ready before you sit in seiza or pick up the brush. You take the position, move through the motion, and the readiness follows the form rather than preceding it.

Sleep inertia rewards the same order of operations. Waiting to feel alert before you get up and get light on your face gets the sequence backwards — the feeling of alertness is downstream of the action, not a precondition for it.

## The Lab Note

Tomorrow, don't negotiate with the fog. Get up at your alarm, and get outside — or at minimum, next to a bright window — within the first ten minutes. Don't wait to feel like it. The form comes first; the clarity follows.
